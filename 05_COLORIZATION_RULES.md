# Purpose

Historical figures deserve more than automatic colorization.

Identity Forge does not simply "add color" to black-and-white references. It reconstructs a believable historical appearance based on evidence, geography, culture, period, material science, and artistic continuity.

The purpose of this document is to define the standards by which Identity Forge transforms monochrome historical references into vibrant, historically grounded, cinematic characters suitable for the Identity Forge Universe.

Color is not decoration.

Color communicates identity, emotion, geography, culture, and story.

Color should also help distinguish one emotional identity from another.

Every color decision should help a child understand and connect with the character while remaining faithful to historical evidence.

---

# Core Philosophy

A black-and-white engraving is not a black-and-white person.

An oil painting with faded pigments is not evidence that muted colors were originally intended.

A marble bust is not evidence that skin was white.

A bronze statue is not evidence that clothing was bronze.

Identity Forge reconstructs the living human being—not the surviving artifact.

---

# The Color Reconstruction Pipeline

Every canonical character follows the same process.

```
Historical References
        │
        ▼
Evidence Collection
        │
        ▼
Material Identification
        │
        ▼
Historical Color Research
        │
        ▼
Environmental Context
        │
        ▼
Confidence Assessment
        │
        ▼
Universe Style Harmonization
        │
        ▼
Canonical Color Palette
```

Colorization occurs only after identity reconstruction has been completed.

---

# Guiding Principle

Identity Forge never asks:

> "What colors look cool?"

It asks:

> "What colors are historically plausible, emotionally engaging, and visually consistent with our cinematic universe?"

It also asks:

> "Does this palette support this person's specific emotional signature?"

# Evidence Hierarchy for Color

Not every source provides reliable color information.

Identity Forge assigns confidence to color evidence.

---

## Tier 1 — Direct Color Evidence

Highest confidence.

Examples:

- Color photographs
- Hand-colored photographs verified by historians
- Preserved garments
- Preserved textiles
- Preserved pigments
- Museum conservation reports

Whenever available, these become primary references.

---

## Tier 2 — Contemporary Paintings

Portraits painted during the subject's lifetime.

Useful for:

- clothing
- jewelry
- fabrics
- eye color
- hair color

Must still be evaluated for artistic bias.

---

## Tier 3 — Historical Scholarship

Museum publications.

Academic historians.

Archaeological reports.

Costume historians.

Textile specialists.

These sources often provide more reliable color information than surviving artwork.

---

## Tier 4 — Cultural Reconstruction

Regional clothing traditions.

Available dyes.

Religious customs.

Professional attire.

Climate.

Trade networks.

These provide reasonable constraints for unknown colors.

---

## Tier 5 — Artistic Interpretation

Lowest confidence.

Used only after all historical evidence has been exhausted.

Must remain historically plausible.

# Reconstructing Skin Tone

Skin tone should never be guessed randomly.

Consider:

- ancestry
- geographic origin
- climate
- historical migration
- available scholarly evidence
- documented ethnicity
- family lineage when known

Identity Forge seeks believable human variation rather than idealized beauty.

Avoid:

- whitening historical figures
- darkening historical figures without evidence
- smoothing natural variation
- exaggerated fantasy skin tones

Children should see real people.

---

# Hair Color

Hair color changes with age.

Identity Forge reconstructs the person during the chosen narrative period.

Examples:

Young Newton:

brown

Older Newton:

gray-white

Leonardo:

gray later in life

Marie Curie:

dark brown transitioning with age

Hair should always reflect:

- age
- historical evidence
- chronology
- documented descriptions

Never choose hair color simply because it creates stronger visual contrast.

---

# Eye Color

Eye color is one of the least certain historical attributes.

When documented:

Preserve it.

When uncertain:

Infer conservatively using available evidence.

When unknown:

Choose the least speculative plausible option and record confidence internally.

Avoid dramatic fantasy eye colors.

Eyes should communicate emotion—not novelty.

---

# Clothing Color

Clothing should reflect:

- available dyes
- wealth
- profession
- social status
- historical period
- regional customs

Examples:

Scholars often wore restrained palettes.

Royal figures may wear richer pigments.

Scientists should not all wear identical brown robes.

Variation is encouraged.

Randomness is not.

---

# Historical Pigments

Identity Forge recognizes that pigments were constrained by technology.

Examples include:

- indigo
- madder
- ochre
- ultramarine
- vermilion
- lead white
- charcoal black
- earth greens

Color reconstruction should respect what was physically possible during the period.

Avoid introducing modern synthetic colors into ancient contexts.

---

# Material Color

Materials possess natural identities.

Wood.

Stone.

Leather.

Metal.

Paper.

Glass.

Fabric.

These should retain physically believable coloration.

Identity Forge renders materials as they would appear in the real world—not as they appear in faded museum artifacts.

---

# Environmental Lighting

Color never exists independently of light.

Identity Forge assumes physically believable illumination.

Examples:

Warm afternoon sunlight.

Soft candlelight.

Library window light.

Workshop illumination.

Observatory moonlight.

The same robe may appear differently under different lighting conditions.

Lighting should enhance emotional readability while remaining believable.

Lighting should also support emotional differentiation, so quiet intensity, joyful obsession, calm wisdom, and restless imagination do not share the same mood by default.

# Color Confidence Levels

Every major color decision should internally receive a confidence rating.

## High Confidence

Supported by direct evidence.

---

## Medium Confidence

Supported by multiple historical indicators.

---

## Low Confidence

Reasonable inference.

Documented.

---

## Unknown

No reliable evidence.

Conservative artistic judgment permitted.

---

# Common Failure Modes

Colorization has failed when:

- colors are chosen randomly
- every scholar wears brown
- every scientist wears blue
- all skin tones become identical
- every beard becomes white
- every ancient culture shares one palette
- museum discoloration is mistaken for historical color
- faded paintings dictate final appearance

---

# Quality Assurance Checklist

Before approving a canonical palette ask:

✓ Does every major color have historical justification?

✓ Does the palette respect geography?

✓ Does it respect chronology?

✓ Does it respect profession?

✓ Does it fit the Identity Forge Universe?

✓ Would a child find the character visually engaging?

✓ Does the palette enhance emotional storytelling?

✓ Does the palette support the character's distinct emotional signature?

✓ Is the character recognizable from color alone?

# Final Principle

Color is one of the first things a child remembers.

A child may forget a date.

A child may forget a theorem.

But they will remember:

Newton's silver hair.

Ada's elegant green dress.

Ibn al-Haytham's warm golden turban.

Marie Curie's deep blue laboratory coat.

Color becomes part of identity.

Identity Forge therefore treats every color decision as part of the historical reconstruction itself—not as a cosmetic finishing step.

The goal is not to paint history.

The goal is to let history feel alive.
