# Purpose

Historical references are not photographs.

Every surviving image of a historical figure carries the fingerprints of the medium that created it.

An engraving contains engraving artifacts.

A marble bust contains sculpture artifacts.

An oil painting contains brushstroke artifacts.

A faded manuscript contains aging artifacts.

Identity Forge's responsibility is to distinguish between the **person** and the **medium**.

The objective is simple:

**Preserve the person. Remove the medium.**

This document defines the standards by which Identity Forge identifies, classifies, and removes source-medium artifacts before canonical reconstruction begins.

---

# Core Philosophy

Identity Forge is not restoring historical artwork.

It is recovering historical identity.

Every artifact that belongs to the artist, engraver, sculptor, photographer, scanner, or museum should be removed if it interferes with understanding the actual human being.

The final canonical character should appear as though the historical person stepped into a modern cinematic camera—not as though a centuries-old artifact was photographed.

---

# Guiding Principle

Every visual feature belongs to one of two categories:

## Identity

Characteristics belonging to the historical person.

Examples:

- skull shape
- facial proportions
- nose
- eyes
- ears
- jaw
- beard style
- hairstyle
- age
- posture
- clothing design
- cultural accessories

Identity must be preserved.

---

## Artifact

Characteristics introduced by the recording medium.

Examples:

- brush strokes
- engraving lines
- marble grain
- cracked varnish
- faded pigments
- paper yellowing
- camera dust
- scanner distortion

Artifacts must be removed.

---

# Artifact Removal Pipeline

Every reference follows the same preprocessing workflow.

```
Historical Reference
        │
        ▼
Artifact Detection
        │
        ▼
Artifact Classification
        │
        ▼
Identity Protection
        │
        ▼
Artifact Removal
        │
        ▼
Visual Validation
        │
        ▼
Canonical Reconstruction
```

Artifact removal always occurs before stylization.

# Artifact Taxonomy

Identity Forge classifies artifacts into distinct categories.

---

# Category 1 — Engraving Artifacts

Common examples:

- hatch lines
- cross hatching
- stippling
- etched shadows
- carved line texture
- woodcut grooves

Purpose of removal:

These lines describe light—not skin.

Do not interpret them as wrinkles.

Do not interpret them as facial hair.

Do not interpret them as anatomy.

---

# Category 2 — Oil Painting Artifacts

Examples:

- visible brush strokes
- impasto
- canvas weave
- paint cracking
- pigment bleed
- glazing artifacts
- varnish discoloration

Purpose:

Recover the underlying person rather than reproducing the painting technique.

---

# Category 3 — Sculpture Artifacts

Examples:

- marble grain
- chisel marks
- stone erosion
- missing noses
- broken ears
- damaged surfaces
- polished highlights

Important Principle:

A marble statue is not evidence of skin texture.

Stone should never become flesh.

---

# Category 4 — Manuscript Artifacts

Examples:

- ink bleed
- parchment texture
- page folds
- stains
- tears
- faded ink
- edge damage

These belong to the document—not the person.

---

# Category 5 — Printing Artifacts

Examples:

- halftone dots
- newspaper texture
- printing misalignment
- offset ink
- low-resolution scans

Modern reproduction errors should never influence reconstruction.

---

# Category 6 — Aging Artifacts

Examples:

- yellowing
- fading
- oxidation
- water damage
- mildew
- discoloration
- foxing
- dust accumulation

These represent time—not history.

---

# Category 7 — Digital Artifacts

Examples:

- JPEG compression
- AI upscaling errors
- sharpening halos
- scanner noise
- aliasing
- pixelation
- color banding

Digital imperfections should never become permanent character features.

---

# Category 8 — Photographic Artifacts

Applicable to early photographs.

Examples:

- scratches
- dust
- chemical damage
- exposure problems
- lens distortion
- motion blur
- film grain

Separate photography defects from physical appearance.

---

# Identity Protection

Artifact removal must never damage identity.

Before removing any visual feature, Identity Forge asks:

Does this belong to the historical person?

or

Does this belong to the recording medium?

If uncertain,

preserve.

Never erase identity for the sake of cleanliness.

---

# Medium-Specific Reconstruction

Each medium requires different interpretation.

---

## Engravings

Extract:

- facial geometry
- clothing
- expression
- proportions

Discard:

- hatch marks
- line shading
- engraved texture

---

## Paintings

Extract:

- composition
- clothing
- facial structure
- posture
- color evidence (when reliable)

Discard:

- brush texture
- craquelure
- varnish shifts
- painterly stylization

---

## Marble Busts

Extract:

- proportions
- bone structure
- silhouette
- hairstyle

Discard:

- marble texture
- chipped edges
- missing features
- polished stone appearance

---

## Coins and Medallions

Extract:

- profile
- nose
- chin
- hair outline
- symbolic accessories

Treat with caution.

Coins exaggerate relief for manufacturing purposes.

---

# Style Bias Detection

Historical artists often imposed stylistic conventions.

Examples:

Idealized kings.

Heroic poses.

Religious symbolism.

Romantic exaggeration.

Neoclassical beauty.

Identity Forge should recognize these conventions and recover the underlying individual rather than reproducing the artistic movement.

---

# Historical Damage Recovery

Some references have suffered physical damage.

Examples:

missing nose

broken ear

burn damage

cropped image

missing eye

paint loss

water damage

Identity Forge reconstructs missing information only when supported by independent evidence.

Never invent unsupported anatomy.

---

# Black-and-White Bias

One of the most important principles.

A monochrome source should never force a monochrome reconstruction.

Similarly,

a grayscale photograph does not imply gray clothing,

gray skin,

gray environment,

or gray lighting.

Black-and-white is a limitation of recording—not reality.

---

# Identity Preservation Rules

During artifact removal,

the following should never change:

- facial proportions
- eye placement
- nose structure
- jaw
- brow
- ears
- hairline
- beard geometry
- age
- expression tendency

Identity always outranks cleanup.

---

# Common Failure Modes

Artifact removal has failed when:

- engraving lines become wrinkles

- brush strokes become skin texture

- marble grain becomes pores

- cracks become scars

- faded pigments become clothing colors

- museum lighting becomes canonical lighting

- damaged statues become damaged people

- artistic exaggeration becomes anatomy

---

# Quality Assurance Checklist

Before approving artifact removal ask:

✓ Does the reconstructed face feel like a living human?

✓ Have all recording-medium artifacts been removed?

✓ Was identity preserved?

✓ Could this person plausibly have been photographed today?

✓ Are there any visible reminders of the original medium?

✓ Does the image feel historically alive rather than historically preserved?

If any answer is no,

continue refinement.

---

# Final Principle

Children should never feel that they are looking through centuries of damage.

They should feel as though they have traveled back in time and met the historical person face-to-face.

The purpose of artifact removal is not perfection.

The purpose is transparency.

When Identity Forge succeeds,

the medium disappears,

the centuries disappear,

and only the person remains.
